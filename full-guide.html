Before the meeting or event

Confirm the room is accessible and meets participants' needs.

How to do this

  • Check the route into the room, including doorways, ramps, elevators, and clear paths for mobility devices
  • Check seating, tables, and sightlines so participants can see speakers, screens, and each other
  • Check access to washrooms, emergency exits, and any safety supports participants may need

Related guidance

  • Planning and testing accessibility services (opens in a new tab)

Helps prevent barriers such as

  • Participants cannot enter, navigate, or leave the space independently.
  • Mobility devices, service animals, or accessibility equipment cannot be accommodated.
  • Seating, tables, or room layouts block access or participation.
  • Participants cannot see, hear, or access key information from their location in the room.
  • Accessibility features or supports are unavailable when needed.

Provide clear location, arrival, room, and accessibility information.

How to do this

  • Share entrance, elevator, washroom, and parking or transit details in the invitation, not just the room number.
  • Include a contact participants can reach if they need help finding or accessing the room.

Helps prevent barriers such as

  • Participants do not know where to go or how to access the room.
  • Participants arrive without information about entrances, elevators, washrooms, parking, or transit access.
  • Participants do not know who to contact if they experience an access issue.
  • Important access information is only shared verbally or too late.

Provide accessible information about emergency procedures, exits, washrooms, and safety supports.

How to do this

  • Point out accessible exits, washrooms, and evacuation routes at the start, not just visually — say it out loud too.
  • Confirm who will assist a participant who cannot evacuate independently, and how.

Helps prevent barriers such as

  • Participants cannot find accessible exits, washrooms, or safety supports.
  • Emergency information is only posted visually or only explained verbally.
  • Participants who need assistance do not know what support is available.
  • Safety instructions are unclear, incomplete, or unavailable in an accessible format.

Confirm and test microphones, speakers, displays, captions, interpretation, or assistive listening supports when needed.

How to do this

  • Test audio and visual equipment in the actual room beforehand, not just on paper.
  • Confirm a backup plan (spare microphone, alternate device) in case equipment fails.

Plan how remote and in-person participants will access the same information, supports, and participation methods.

How to do this

  • Map out, before the event, exactly how each piece of information (slides, questions, decisions) will reach both audiences equally.
  • Avoid designing an activity that only works for people in the room, or only for people online.

Test all accessibility features, materials, and technology before the meeting or event, using the actual setup (room audio, microphones, speakers, cameras, captions, interpretation, screen sharing, and remote access).

How to do this

  • Test the full setup together, not each piece separately, confirm remote participants can hear in-room speakers and vice versa if hybrid.
  • Test captions, interpretation, audio, screen sharing, and participation tools together, using the actual event setup.
  • For public, recorded, high-profile, or high-risk events, consider involving accessibility specialists or test users.

Assign dedicated support roles for the room, virtual platform, accessibility services, questions, and technical issues.

How to do this

  • Assign separate people to monitor the room and the virtual platform — one person usually can't do both.
  • Make sure someone is specifically responsible for accessibility services (captions, interpretation) during the event.

  • Questions, chat messages, raised hands, or support requests are missed.
  • Accessibility or technical issues are reported but no one is assigned to respond.
  • One person is expected to manage too many tasks at once.

Prepare a backup plan for anything that could fail: audio (in the room and/or online, depending on your delivery format), captions, interpretation, participation tools, the platform or livestream, or a booked accessibility service provider.

How to do this

  • Decide in advance who will troubleshoot and how you'll notify participants if something fails.
  • For technical failures, audio (in-room, online, or both), captions, interpretation, participation tools, platform, or livestream, have a fallback ready (phone line, alternate device, or secondary streaming link) so the event doesn't stall while you fix the issue.
  • For booked accessibility services (captioning, interpretation), line up a standby provider you can call on short notice.
  • Decide in advance who is authorized to make the call to pause, switch, or reschedule.

Helps prevent barriers such as

  • Organizers cannot quickly switch to another way of sharing information or supporting participation.
  • A vendor cancellation, technical failure, or livestream outage leaves the event without a needed service and no standby ready.
  • Participants do not know what to do when access issues occur.

Identify who will host or support the meeting, even if one person performs multiple roles.

How to do this

  • Make sure there's a backup if that person is unavailable.

How to do this

  • Provide important event information early enough for participants to request accommodations, review materials, and prepare to participate.
